What it is

Murmuration reads public internet records and looks for traces of AI agents acting together. The records are ones that services already publish: wiki change logs, package registries, notification channels, web-archive captures, game listings and open feeds. One person runs it, on a laptop and on rented machines for the heavy jobs.

The page does not try to prove who is behind anything. It answers a narrower question: does a pattern in a public record look like many automated accounts working at once, and does it still look that way after a fair comparison against ordinary activity.

Why it exists

In 2026 several incidents were reported in which groups of agents used ordinary public services during tests, a small wiki, message channels and echo services among them, to leave and pick up information. The best documented one is at collusion.wiki. Cases like these have mostly been found by people who already had server logs with network addresses in them.

Most people have no such logs. The observatory asks what can be seen from the outside using only what anyone is allowed to read. Two cases were found that way. A larger number of leads did not survive a control, and those are published too, because a list of dead ends saves other people the work.

The five labels

Confirmed
The pattern passed its frozen rule and every control, and it lines up with an independent record or with published documentation.
Open lead
The pattern passed at least one frozen rule and beat at least one control. It has not been confirmed and may still turn out to be coincidence. Nothing on the page rules a swarm in or out for these.
Found, not covert
A real pattern with an ordinary explanation, such as one account publishing a package family or an open program that labels its own work.
Closed
The test found nothing, or the pattern disappeared under a control.
Incomplete
The data or the coverage was not good enough to score. These say nothing either way.

How a test runs

  1. A source and a question are chosen, for example: does any day show many new single-use names at once.
  2. The scoring rule is written down. It is committed to the repository together with its SHA-256 before any data is scored.
  3. The data is read and scored with that rule only. A rule changed after seeing the data is labelled post-hoc and counts for less.
  4. The result is compared with a control.
  5. The outcome is recorded whether it is a hit or not, with the numbers.
  6. Identifiers are hashed on reading. Only counts and structure are kept.
  7. A person reads the page before it is made public.

Controls

A pattern only counts if it is larger than what a control produces. The controls used are listed here, with the situation each fits.

ControlWhat it doesUsed when
Ordinary dayScores days with no known event by the same rule.Bursts in a daily count.
ShuffleMoves events to random times within the same day or the same hour and scores again, 1,000 to 10,000 times.Groups of events close in time.
Circular shiftRotates one source's timeline by a random amount and checks how often two sources still line up.Two records flagging the same day.
Activity-matchedDraws comparison accounts or groups with the same volume as the pattern.Groups of accounts or owners.
Degree-preservingRewires reply links at random while every account keeps its number of links.Reply chains between accounts.

The p-value is the share of control draws that reach the observed value. With 2,000 draws the smallest possible value is about 0.0005. A p-value of 1 means the control reaches the observed value every time. A global p-value adjusts for the number of places and days that were searched, since a rare result is expected somewhere if enough places are checked.

Reading the charts

Bar and line charts show counts over time. A dashed vertical line marks a date discussed in the text.

Dot plots compare each test with its control. The dot is the largest value observed. The grey bar is the range the control produced between its median and its 99th percentile. A dot inside or on the end of the bar means the control explains the result. A dot clearly past the bar means the control does not.

Every chart has a table underneath it with the same numbers.

Words used

Agent
A program driven by an AI model that takes actions, for example fetching pages or editing a wiki.
Swarm
Many agents acting at the same time with signs that they affect each other.
Burst day
A day that meets the frozen rule for an unusual number of new accounts, names or items.
Machine-named
A name that matches a frozen pattern for generated names, such as a random string or a template with a counter.
Single-use
A name or channel seen once and never again.
K
The number of independent records that flag the same calendar day. K=2 means two sources flagged it.
Post-hoc
A rule or check written after the data was seen.
Coverage
How much of the period a source actually returned. Low coverage makes a null result weak.
ASN
The network operator a public address belongs to. Used only as a count per operator.

What is kept

Counts, dates, hashed identifiers and the scoring code. Page text, post text, usernames and addresses are not kept. Identifiers are keyed and hashed in memory when read, and the key is dropped. Network addresses, where a source includes them, are reduced to the operator they belong to. Nothing is linked to a named person, lab or company, and a similar pattern is not read as evidence about who was behind it.

Datasets that others released under terms are used within those terms and are not republished.

Limits

  • Public sources lose history. Some feeds only keep recent items, so an empty result for an old date can mean the record is gone.
  • A control that cannot move the pattern cannot dismantle it either. Where this happened it is stated next to the result.
  • Many results are single-day or single-source. They are leads for further tests and not findings.
  • Code, drafts and several of the test runs were produced with AI assistants. The rules, the numbers and the wording are checked by a person before anything is published.
  • One reader checks everything, so errors are possible. Corrections are welcome and are listed on the overview page when made.
