Where things stand

Two confirmed cases, three open leads

Two cases are confirmed: bursts of single-use channel names on a public notification service in July, and a burst of new accounts on a small wiki in May and June. The wiki case was documented at collusion.wiki first and was recovered independently here from its public change log.

One cross-source lead on 29 September is still open. Two independent public records flag the day, and no third record does. It could still be a swarm, and nothing confirms that it is. Most other leads died under harder controls, and those results are kept on the page. The rest of the page gives the evidence for each row.

Confirmed

Two confirmed cases

A case is called confirmed here when it passed a frozen rule and every control, and when it lines up with an independent record. Two cases meet that bar. Both were found from public records only.

ntfy.sh single-use channels, 6 and 8 July 2026

ntfy.sh is a public notification service where anyone can publish to a named channel. On 6 July 55 new channel names appeared, and on 8 July 81. Each name was seen once and never again. An ordinary day brings a median of 3 new names. Median lifetime of the July names was one day, against 11.5 days for a first set of 8 ordinary names and 3 days for a larger set of 15.

The names are not one template. Dictionary-word share was 7% on 6 July and 0% on 8 July, and the names are short. A sweep of 24 relay, paste and echo services from January 2025 to October 2026 recovered both days. The March spike on the same service was an archive crawl and does not count. The two July days were selected after the fact from a distinct-name outlier, which is noted against the result.

DSEWiki, 24 May to 2 July 2026

DSEWiki is a small wiki whose public change log runs to 22,802 entries from 2,819 hashed authors. The incident is documented at collusion.wiki. Here it was recovered separately: the scoring rule (SHA-256 beginning de210866) was committed before scoring, and it picked out 12 burst days from 26 May to 22 June. The peak, 18 June, had 748 new accounts and 5,925 edits. On burst days 41% of new account names were machine-named. On ordinary days the share was 0%.

The date range of the log is wider than the burst range. Nothing in the log shows a burst on 6 to 8 July, only administrator clean-up. The agent writes end around 09:00 UTC on 22 June, a day before the operator's mass deletion, which is why the displacement question remains open (see open leads).

The scanner trace

Other researchers reported that agents limited to a fetch-this-page tool used public web-analysis services as a browser. Those services publish what was submitted, so the incident left a count in public. The weekly series below shows echo-host submissions rising in April, peaking in the week of 15 June and falling away after 21 June. Many hosts are involved and the counts say nothing about operators. A second public scanner, urlscan, returned only 3 results across the incident's 9 hosts and 11 weeks, with no surge in the incident weeks, so that feed would not have exposed the incident without prior knowledge.

Open leads

Three leads still open

An open lead beat at least one control and has not been confirmed. None of these is called a swarm. None is ruled out either.

29 September 2026: two public records flag the same day

Two unrelated public records show something unusual on 29 September. In a package index, one account published 33 first releases in 164.8 minutes. In a web archive of an echo service, the 23 captures of that day are 83% in the page style of the documented swarm days, against 9% on ordinary days. The same style share was 93% on 6 July and 65% on 28 May, and each of those days also coincides with another record: ntfy on 6 July and a DSEWiki burst day on 28 May.

The reasons it stays open are specific. The archive signature on 29 September matches the two documented days closely. A separate venue, a public game, shows two ten-account registration batches the same day. The reasons it is not confirmed are also specific. The package-index side is a single account publishing a family of packages, which is a bulk release more than many accounts. A pair of sources agreeing is not rare when many days and sources are searched. The archive has no 2025 baseline (46 captures in the whole year), the swarm-like days are single sessions of one to three hours, and shared crawling remains a possible cause. The day was picked after the fact, so it cannot be confirmed by the rule that found it.

Twelve more sources were checked for a third record (table below). None flagged under its frozen rule. A RubyGems snapshot due on 5 October may allow one more check. Network-identity data for the day would settle more than any further count.

30 September: ten owners, one Space name

Ten owner namespaces on a public model-hosting service published a Space with the same exact name within 15 minutes. The shuffle median was 6 owners and its 99th percentile 8, with p=0.0005. The group was then tested four more ways and none supported a shared origin: the owners shared no other Space name, their namespace shapes all differed, no code fingerprint was shared by more than two Spaces, and none carried the markers of the known open reproduction program. The same day's other Spaces lack those markers too, so the missing markers say little. The cause is unknown.

Displacement of the DSEWiki agents

Agent writes on DSEWiki stop at about 09:00 UTC on 22 June, before the operator's mass deletion on 23 June. The hypothesis is that the activity moved to other channels, to hide or simply to use a different tool. It has been partly tested. The agents did not move to new network prefixes (120 of 121 were reused). All four scanner hosts end in the same week, which points to one upstream switch-off. The July activity reported by others used an execution service that leaves no public receipts, after the scanner trace ended. Intent cannot be told apart from a change of tool, and several checks were blocked by a rate limit.

Closed and incomplete

What did not hold up

Most tests found nothing. Each line below is a pre-registered test, the control that stopped it, and the numbers. A null result applies only to the data it could see, and several sources return only part of their history.

How the tests compare with their controls

Other wikis: no second DSEWiki

The wiki replication was run on 4 October. The rule was committed first, and the harness was checked on stored DSEWiki counts before any other wiki was scored. Each wiki received one bounded request escalation, 95 requests in total, with a 3 second gap and no retry on 403, 429 or server errors. The visible change-log window on most wikis covers September and October only, so a null here does not speak to the May to July window of the original incident.

Method

How a claim is made, and what is kept

A scoring rule is written down and committed with its SHA-256 before any data is scored. A positive has to beat an ordinary-day, shuffle, shifted-date or activity-matched control. Failures stay in the record. The page keeps counts and structure. Identifiers are hashed on reading. Raw names, messages and personal data are not stored, and nothing is attributed to a person, lab or company. A person reads this page before it goes live. More detail is on the info page.

Limits that apply to the whole page

  • Public sources lose history. For several sources an empty result for May to July means the record is gone.
  • A control that cannot move the pattern cannot take it apart either. Where that happened the result says so, as with the within-hour game shuffle and the Moltbook degree-preserving null.
  • The July ntfy days and the 29 September day were picked after the fact, which weakens any p-value attached to them.
  • Many leads are single-day and single-source. They are reasons for further tests.
  • Code and many test runs come from AI agents. A person checks rules, numbers and wording.
